# Entity and Ownership Design

Use a Texas LLC to buy and develop the tract, then deed each homesite to its household and hold the common tract in a recorded property owners' association (POA). Do not form a Chapter 251 cooperative association, and do not take title as tenants in common. Part of [[Friendstead]]; sources in [[Friendstead Sources]].

Three findings sharpen the [[Texas Self-Build Community Structure Memo|source memo]]: an LLC cannot hold a homestead, a POA beats a second LLC for the common tract, and the 10-acre platting exemption is a real alternative worth pricing.

## Best fit for each design question

| Design question | Best fit | Why, in one line |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns the raw land during development | Development LLC, manager-managed, one member per household | Blocks partition suits, one borrower and one seller, company agreement carries capital-call, exit and voting rules |
| Who owns each finished homesite | Each household in fee simple, by deed from the LLC after plat | Needed for homestead exemption, construction mortgages, inheritance and clean resale |
| Who owns the common tract, road, well and barndo | A POA under Property Code ch. 209 with recorded declaration and easements, membership running with each lot | Assessments become liens that survive sale; a second LLC would need a new buy-in contract at every resale |
| How money and labor are contributed | Company agreement for cash; a separate Mutual Construction Labor Agreement counted in hours, never dollars; a shortfall raises that household's cash share of shared development | Hours traded among friends with no price and no cash settlement are not taxable barter; money only ever moves to the LLC or POA, never between households |
| Shared barndominium | Phase 1 LLC asset, permitted for residential occupancy from day one; conveyed to the POA at plat | A workshop permit will not lawfully house eight people, and septic sizing follows the permit |

## Candidates compared

| Vehicle | Partition risk | Homestead and financing on finished homes | Assessments survive resale | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tenancy in common from day one | High: any co-owner may compel partition, Prop. Code § 23.001 **[S14]** | Undivided shares, so no lender finances one house and no homestead attaches to a lot | No | Reject |
| Texas LLC for the whole project, members keep interests forever | None | Fails: Tax Code § 11.13(j)(1) limits the residence homestead to property owned by individuals or a qualifying trust **[S12]**; no exemption, no creditor protection, no conventional mortgage | Yes, by contract | Development phase only |
| Chapter 251 cooperative association | None | Same failure; locked to one member one vote by BOC § 251.254, and any device to evade it is unenforceable **[S13]** | Yes | Reject |
| Condominium regime (Prop. Code ch. 82) | None | Units financeable and homestead-eligible, but rural detached condos are nonstandard to lenders | Yes, statutory lien | Fallback if the county will not plat |
| Development LLC → plat → fee-simple lots plus Chapter 209 POA | None | Full: each household takes a deed and files its own homestead | Yes, recorded declaration and assessment lien | **Recommended** |

## The five pieces

1. **Raw land during development: a manager-managed Texas LLC.** Capital accounts per household, two or three managers elected annually. The company agreement carries the capital-call ladder, voting thresholds, buyout formula and the schedule of future homesite rights. Members' interests are personal property, which removes partition risk and lets the LLC be the single borrower.
2. **Finished homesites: fee simple by deed from the LLC**, only after the plat records and the household's cash and labor accounts are square. One Texas lender states the homeowner cannot act as contractor when the home will be homestead property **[S41]**, so plan a builder of record or a lender that accepts owner-builders.
3. **Common tract, road, well and barndominium: a Chapter 209 POA.** Recorded declaration ties membership to lot ownership, makes assessments a lien, and gives the right of first refusal teeth. Keep it short: road, water, barndo, gate, drainage, reserve study every five years.
4. **Member money and labor: two documents, and labor is never priced in dollars.** Company agreement for cash. A Mutual Construction Labor Agreement sets an hour target per household, the tasks that count, who may do licensed work (nobody without a license), the safety rules and the insurance program. The ledger is kept in hours only; a shortfall raises that household's cash share of shared development by a formula in the company agreement, and money never passes between households for hours. See [[Member Labor Matrix]] and *Labor without a tax bill* below.
5. **Shared barndominium: LLC asset in Phase 1, POA asset after plat, permitted as a residence from the first permit.** Size the septic for the Phase 1 occupant count.

Who votes on what inside the LLC and the POA, and how a household leaves at each stage, is in [[Governance and Exit]].

## The 10-acre alternative

Loc. Gov't Code § 232.0015(f) bars a county from requiring a plat when every lot is more than 10 acres and no street is laid out for public dedication **[S11]**. Three households on 33 acres, or four on 44, could divide by deed and recorded easements with no plat, no county road standard and no subdivision review. The trade is more land per household and fewer households to share fixed costs. Hays and Bastrop both honor the exemption; each still requires an OSSF permit and 911 addressing **[S8]**. Run this case alongside the six-household base case before choosing a tract **[J]**.

## Labor without a tax bill

Labor is counted in hours and never priced in dollars, so no household ever pays another for work and no taxable barter arises. Work on your own house is not income. Partners working on their own partnership's property without pay are not its employees (Rev. Rul. 69-184) **[S75]**. Income appears only when services are exchanged at a stated value: the source memo's labor units with a credit rate and a cash true-up would create exactly that record, and a club that issues tradeable labor credits is a barter exchange under Treas. Reg. § 1.6045-1(a)(4) with its own reporting duties **[S73]**, while the same regulation excludes "the informal exchange of similar services on a noncommercial basis". That design is dropped (decided 2026-10-23).

**The chosen design (Path B)**

1. Lots deed at plat and each household takes its own construction loan.
2. The group builds together. The labor agreement sets an hour target per household and a ledger kept in hours only, with no dollar conversion anywhere in the documents.
3. A household short of its target pays a larger cash share of shared development (road, well, barndominium) by a formula in the company agreement. That is a capital contribution to the household's own entity, not payment for services, and it lowers everyone else's call.
4. Work on the common tract after plat is volunteer work for the POA.
5. Enforcement stays where it already is: the capital-call ladder and the lien contract under [Prop. Code § 53.254](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PR/htm/PR.53.htm#53.254).

**The alternative (Path A).** The LLC builds all six houses with member labor and distributes each finished lot and house in kind, generally tax-free (IRC § 731) with carryover basis (§ 732). The sweat equity sits as unrealized gain inside a homestead, and § 121 excludes up to $500,000 of that gain for a couple on a sale after two years of residence. Cleaner on tax, but it needs one construction loan for six houses with member guarantees and delays every homestead until completion. Kept as the fallback if lenders will not finance owner-builders.

A CPA should sign off on the hours-only design and on the timing of cash in and lot out (disguised-sale rules, IRC § 707(a)(2)(B)) before the term sheet goes to counsel. Planning on what the code allows, not tax advice.

## Liability protection for the properties

Each household's home ends up behind the Texas homestead shield, the LLC and the POA keep shared assets off anyone's personal balance sheet, and insurance covers what the law does not.

| Layer | What protects it | Source | What it does not cover |
|---|---|---|---|
| Each home and lot after the deed | Rural homestead of up to 200 acres for a family, no dollar cap, exempt from forced sale by general creditors | [Tex. Const. art. XVI § 50](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CN/htm/CN.16.htm#16.50) **[S74]**; [Prop. Code § 41.002](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._prop._code_section_41.002) **[S63]** | Purchase-money and home-equity liens, property taxes, a mechanic's lien signed under § 53.254, federal tax liens |
| The LLC's land and barndominium during development | Members are not liable for the LLC's debts; a member's personal creditor gets only a charging order on distributions, never the land | [BOC § 101.114](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._orgs._code_section_101.114) **[S64]**; [§ 101.112](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._orgs._code_section_101.112) **[S65]** | Loan guarantees members sign; a member's own negligence on a work crew |
| Road, well, barndominium and common tract after plat | Owned by the POA, a nonprofit corporation; members are not liable for its obligations; assessments fund the premiums | [BOC § 22.152](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._orgs._code_section_22.152) **[S66]**; Prop. Code ch. 209 | A member's own acts; an underinsured claim comes back as an assessment |
| Visitors on the common tract and in the shop | Recreational-use statute limits liability to gross negligence; signed releases for shop and equipment use that meet the fair-notice rules | [CPRC §§ 75.002–75.003](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._civ._practice_and_remedies_code_section_75.002) **[S67]** (applies to agricultural land for invited guests and to any land entered free of charge); Dresser Industries v. Page Petroleum (Tex. 1993) **[S76]** | Gross negligence; a parent cannot release a child's claim in advance |
| Hired trades on site | A property owner is not liable for a contractor's employee's injury unless it controlled the work and knew of the danger | [CPRC § 95.003](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._civ._practice_and_remedies_code_section_95.003) **[S68]** | Member crews, who are nobody's employees: occupational accident policy instead |
| Members working on the build | The LLC is not an employer and no member is its employee. Members contribute hours as owners under the company agreement, not under a contract of hire: no wages, no hourly rate, no one directing another's work as a boss, and licensed trades come in as independent contractors. The Labor Code defines an employee as a person in the service of another under a contract of hire ([§ 401.012](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._labor_code_section_401.012)), so the paperwork says in plain words, in the company agreement, the labor exhibit, the safety rules and the releases, that hours are an owner's contribution and create no employment. Members release each other as co-owners in a release that meets the *Dresser* fair-notice rules, and occupational accident cover is the backstop **[S78]** **[S76]** | If a court found a contract of hire anyway, the LLC would be a workers' comp nonsubscriber: it would lose the contributory-negligence, assumption-of-risk and fellow-servant defences and any pre-injury waiver by the member would be void ([§ 406.033](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._labor_code_section_406.033)). That is why the non-employee footing has to be explicit everywhere, not implied |
| Insurance | LLC: general liability, builder's risk, occupational accident, umbrella. POA: general liability, property cover on the barndominium, directors and officers, umbrella. Household: homeowner's with liability plus a personal umbrella | Broker's written program before the first work day | Intentional acts; business use of the shop beyond hobby scale |

Two rules follow. Do not put a finished home into an LLC for asset protection: an LLC cannot hold a homestead (Tax Code § 11.13(j)(1)), so it would trade the strongest shield in Texas for a weaker one. Keep the shop a POA asset with written rules and signed releases, because a workshop with power tools is the likeliest place for an injury claim.
