# Build Systems Compared

The barndominium is the cheapest 2,000 sq ft home on every path, at roughly $215,000 to $230,000 co-op built against $290,000 to $335,000 for stick-built, $340,000 to $385,000 for a light-gauge steel frame kit, and $430,000 to $465,000 for timber frame with SIPs **[M]**. Stick-built gives members the most labor to displace, because framing and finishes are 54% of its cost and need no license **[S6]**. The steel kit buys termite immunity and straight walls for about $50,000 more than stick, not a saving. The timber frame is an aesthetic choice, not a financial one. Part of [[Friendstead]]; energy in [[Energy Costs by Envelope]]; who does what in [[Member Labor Matrix]]; the same systems taken to net zero in [[Cost-Effective Net-Zero Design]].

All figures are for the same 2,000 sq ft, three-bedroom, standard-finish home on a slab east of Austin, excluding land, well, septic and driveway. Contractor cost is the all-in price a general contractor would charge; construction cost removes an 18% contractor fee and overhead; the labor and materials split uses the 2026 RSMeans-based shares (labor 38 to 42% of a stick-built home) **[S19]** and Bob Vila's 35% labor share for barndominiums **[S20]**.

**How to read it.** Crew labor at a loaded $40 an hour makes $90,000 about 2,250 crew hours; amateurs run 1.3 to 1.5 times slower, so about 3,000 hours per house. Six houses is 18,000 hours, or 3,000 per household over the program. At 20 hours a week that is three years of weekends. The 1,500-hour row is the realistic pledge for households holding jobs **[J]**. What a member hour saves depends on the task: about $80 on average, $125 to $135 on shell work and $50 to $70 on finishes, going by the Cedar's bid-priced labor counter **[S86]**; the cost model's earlier $40 to $80 was a wage, not a bid price.

**Package prices found:** timber frame kit $60 to $90 per sq ft and turnkey $250 to $500 **[S17]**; SIP-only package from $129 per sq ft and timber frame package from $269 per sq ft **[S18]**. The SIP and timber-frame contractor figures are approximate; no Texas 2026 bid was found.


## Cross-check against a documented build

Transcript and every on-screen figure are in the repo at `sources/2026-10-24-mr-post-frame-cedar-cost-breakdown.md`. Line items from the video worth carrying into the barndominium budget, Iowa 2024 prices: structure package materials $35,488 plus porch $12,155, windows $8,366, doors $15,693; slab 55 yd at $32,700 all-in; 3 in closed-cell foam on walls and ceiling $30,450; interior framing materials $12,788; septic (sand filter) $15,800; plumbing $10,000; electrical $14,000; HVAC $24,000 (five-head mini-split plus hydronic boiler); drywall $26,400; cabinets $13,884; permits about $1,500 rural. The v5 estimate of about $256,000 cash for 2,800 sq ft was within 10% of the Cedar's self-build figure for a slightly smaller but more finished building. The v6 brief (24 Oct 2026) is a different product: a 56 x 40 ft, 20 ft eave shell with two 12 x 12 insulated doors, foam, a member-built second floor and temporary rooms, about 3,500 sq ft, estimated line by line with quantities and 2026 unit prices (`designs/barndominium/ESTIMATE-v6.md`) at $171,000 for the shell, foam, floor and rooms and $213,000 with electrical, plumbing and mini-splits; $188,000 and $234,000 with 10% contingency **[M]** **[S118]** **[S119]** **[S120]**. Its shell subtotal is $76 per sq ft of slab against the Cedar's $65 for phase 1, the difference being 2026 prices, 20 ft walls and the code's thermal-barrier coating over the foam. Carry $234,000 until a frame quote arrives; the takeoff is generated by `notes/barndo-v6-estimate.py`.

**What the cross-check changes.** The totals stand: our contractor and self-build figures are within 10% of a documented build once Texas 2026 against Iowa 2024 is allowed for, and the saving percentage is the same. Three things are revised:

- **Value of a member hour.** The model priced displaced labor at a $40 loaded wage, so the saving came out at $40 to $80 an hour. The Cedar's counter prices the builder's labor at what a bid charges, about $178 an hour of skilled time. Allowing for amateurs at 1.3 to 1.5 times slower, a member hour on shell work is worth $125 to $135, on finishes $50 to $70, and about $80 across a whole house. The cost-model row now says so.
- **Post-frame opens the shell to members.** The red-iron model hands the shell to the vendor's crew and leaves members $57,000 of doable work. The Cedar's shell, porch and interior framing were erected by one builder with a telehandler, $75,000 of bid labor in 421 hours. On a post-frame building members can take the shell too, about $105,000 of doable work at bid prices on a 2,000 sq ft house. The co-op total does not move much, because the Cedar's self-build figure still pays the slab and foam crews with their fee and 60% of the trades, but the hours are more useful and less of the build waits on a crane. Decide post-frame against red iron before the frame quotes; get both.
- **Hours.** 421 skilled hours for a dried-in 2,560 sq ft shell with slab prep and interior framing is the first measured figure in the notes. It puts the whole-house amateur estimate at 1,400 to 1,900 hours rather than a flat 1,900.

**Other points from the video worth carrying.** Rural subs charge more because of travel, so budget the trades above metro quotes. Permits ran about $1,500 in rural Iowa against $30,000 for patrons inside city limits; the county-versus-ETJ question in [[Jurisdiction and Feasibility]] is the same lever here. The septic type follows the perc test (conventional, sand filter or peat), which is why the model carries an aerobic system for the east side. Heating and cooling was a five-head mini-split plus a hydronic slab, $24,000 together. 3 in of closed-cell foam on walls and ceiling was $30,450, about $12 per sq ft of footprint, a subbed item on every build. The builders took 10 to 12 years of planning and remodelling before their own self-build and did 95% of it themselves; their first question for anyone is which trades they can honestly do. Their self-build removes the builder's labor only; concrete, foam, septic and 60% of the trades are still paid at pro rates.

## Steel frame kit, how the number was built

This is cold-formed, light-gauge steel framing (studs, tracks, panels, trusses), pre-cut and pre-punched by a fabricator from the house plans and assembled with screws or bolts. It is a different product from the barndominium's red-iron pre-engineered shell: the steel kit replaces the lumber in an otherwise conventional house, so foundation, sheathing, roofing, windows, trades and finishes are all the same as stick-built.

- **Kit price.** HomeAdvisor puts a metal home kit at $20,000 to $90,000 with $25,000 to $50,000 of assembly, $50 to $120 per sq ft for the shell **[S58]**. Blue Sky Building Systems quoted $15 to $33 per sq ft for its bolted frame alone **[S59]**. Steel mill products are up 107% since January 2020 and 28% in the last year ([[Building Materials Price History]]), so older guides understate 2026 prices. The model uses **$60,000 delivered with stamped engineering** for 2,000 sq ft, about $30 per sq ft **[M]**. Kodiak Steel Homes is the best-known residential kit maker; its pricing pages were offline on 22 Oct 2026, so this needs a quote.
- **What it displaces.** About $35,000 of lumber framing material and trusses in the stick-built budget, so net materials rise by about $25,000.
- **Continuous insulation.** Steel studs halve a batt wall's effective R-value: ASHRAE 90.1 rates a nominal R-11 steel-stud wall at about R-5.5, and going from R-13 to R-19 batts adds only about R-1.1 **[S60]**. Exterior rigid foam is the fix and adds about $7,000 of material and $3,000 of labor to this house **[M]**.
- **Labor.** HUD's 2002 Beaufort demonstration found framer hours 4.3% higher than wood and the steel house 14.2% dearer overall; the Valparaiso site in the same series ran framing hours 6.9% higher and the framing package 7.4% dearer **[S61]**. NAHB's researcher put the first-house penalty at 15 to 20% and said it vanishes with the learning curve. For members, a pre-cut, labeled kit removes cutting and layout, which is where amateur framing goes slowest, so member hours are modeled slightly below stick-built despite the screw fastening **[J]**.
- **Trades.** Electricians and plumbers charge a little more on steel (grommets at every penetration, self-drilling fasteners); modeled at 3% on rough-ins. Drywall is unchanged.
- **Why Texas might still choose it.** Termites, straight and plumb walls that stay that way, non-combustible framing, wind ratings in the stamp, and no lumber-price risk. Against it: the steel price is at a record, no local framers know it, exterior foam complicates siding and window details, and the whole-house saving on energy is zero at best.

**Timber frame, two ways.** The timber row above prices a complete frame-plus-SIP kit. A Texas fabricator sells the frame alone at $50,000 to $120,000, and a porch-and-ceiling accent package at about $88,000 on a stick-framed house, which lands near $370,000 on the co-op path **[M]**. See [[Building Materials Price History]] for the reconciliation and for where material prices have gone since 2020.

**Making the panels.** Factory panels alone are about $40,000 for this house, not the package price; laminating them at home would save about $25,000 and forfeit engineering, lender and insurer acceptance. See [[Self-Made SIP Panels]].
