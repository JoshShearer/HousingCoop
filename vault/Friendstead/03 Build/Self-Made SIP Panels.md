# Self-Made SIP Panels

Do not self-manufacture structural SIPs. The raw materials are cheap and lamination is not hard, but no engineer, lender or insurer will accept a structural panel without a tested adhesive bond, and the saving is about $25,000 on a house where members already displace $90,000 of labor another way **[J]**. Buy panels direct from a Texas plant for the roof if the group wants SIPs; build walls as double-stud with cellulose if the goal is the SIP envelope at member-labor cost. Part of [[Friendstead]]; see [[Build Systems Compared]] and [[Building Materials Price History]].

## The price that prompted the question is not the panel price

Trinity's $129 per square foot of floor area **[S18]** is a whole material package: panels plus windows, doors, roofing, trim and design. Panel-only pricing from makers runs about $7 to $12 per square foot of panel, roughly $310 for a 4x8 sheet **[S47]**. A 2,000 sq ft house needs about 4,400 sq ft of wall and roof panel, about 140 sheets, so factory panels alone are on the order of **$40,000** **[M]**.

## What a home-laminated panel costs

Per 4x8 panel with a 6.5 inch core, from retail listings **[M]**:

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Two sheets 7/16 OSB | about $35 |
| 5.5 inch Type I EPS, 4x8 | about $55 to $75; a 2 inch 2 lb sheet lists at $44 **[S48]**; thick sheets ship badly, so buy from a Texas molder; EPS resin rose again in April 2026 **[S49]** |
| Urethane laminating adhesive | about $10 |
| **Total** | **about $100 to $120**, against about $310 factory |

Across 140 panels: roughly $16,000 of materials against $42,000, a saving near $25,000 before the press, waste, and 200 to 300 hours of member labor **[M]**.

## Why it is not a safe plan

- **Code and engineering.** Factory panels carry ICC-ES evaluation reports that specify the OSB grade (7/16, rated 24/16) and an adhesive tested to the AC05 sandwich-panel criteria **[S50]**. A homemade panel has no report, so under IRC R301.1.3 the whole wall and roof system needs an engineered design **[S51]**. Texas counties have no building code, so the gate is not an inspector. It is the engineer, who will want bond and bending tests on the actual panels, and the chapter 233 inspector in counties that opted in (see [[Tax and Code Savings]]).
- **Lender and insurer.** A construction lender's appraiser and a homeowner's insurer will treat a self-laminated structural shell as nonstandard. The co-op already carries financing risk ([[Friendstead Risks]]); this adds to it.
- **Process.** Factory lines press at about 4 to 6 psi for four to five minutes with a foaming urethane adhesive **[S52]**. A vacuum bag or a weighted stack can do it, but bond consistency across 140 panels is where amateurs fail, and a delaminated roof panel fails in the worst place.
- **Connections.** The value of a SIP system is the splines, cam locks, pre-cut openings and electrical chases, not the sandwich. Cutting those by hand is the slow part.

## Ways to capture most of the saving legally

1. **Buy direct from a plant.** R-Control SIPS in Hillsboro, Texas molds its own EPS and laminates panels there **[S53]**, so the group skips the kit packager's margin and most freight. A whole-house SIP operation was also reported moving to New Braunfels; confirm it is operating **[S54]**. Members set the panels; see [[Member Labor Matrix]].
2. **Roof only.** Put factory SIPs where they pay most, over a timber frame or trusses, and stick-frame the walls with member labor.
3. **Site-built equivalent.** A double-stud or Larsen-truss wall with dense-pack cellulose gives the same R-value and air-tightness, uses conventional lumber and conventional engineering, and is all member labor. This is the honest DIY version of a SIP **[J]**.
4. **Non-structural panels.** If someone wants to laminate, do it for nailbase roof insulation over structural decking or for interior partitions, where failure costs little and no engineer cares.
